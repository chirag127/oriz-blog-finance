The pitch is seductive because it sounds like arithmetic. Public-sector
undertakings — the government-owned oil marketers, banks, miners, power and coal
companies — screen absurdly cheap. Price-to-earnings under 6. Price-to-book
under 1. Many pay fat dividends. So the plan writes itself: **buy every PSU
trading at PE < 6 and PB < 1, do it on Margin Trading Facility (MTF) so your
capital works twice as hard, and just hold.** Cheap stocks bought with borrowed
money, held through the cycle. What could go wrong?

Almost everything, and not for the reasons most people fear. This is not a "be
careful, markets are risky" hand-wave. There is a specific, well-documented body
of factor-investing evidence that speaks *directly* to this exact bet — a
leveraged basket of the cheapest available stocks — and its verdict is close to
brutal. The single most famous case of a value investor successfully using
leverage is **Warren Buffett**, and when academics reverse-engineered how he did
it, they found he did nearly the *opposite* of what the cheap-PSU-on-MTF plan
does. The plan keeps the one leg of Buffett's formula that is easy to copy and
discards the three that actually made it work.

This post answers one question honestly: **why shouldn't I just buy every
PE < 6 / PB < 1 PSU stock available on MTF and hold?** The answer is not "cheap
stocks are bad" — deep value is a real, studied premium. The answer is that
*cheapness alone, leveraged, in one correlated corner of the market* is a
near-perfect recipe for the tail risk that leverage punishes hardest. Let's walk
the evidence.

## Why the instinct feels right — and why that's the trap

The plan feels right because every individual step is defensible in isolation:

- **Value works.** Buying cheap earnings and cheap assets is one of the
  best-documented return premia in finance. Over long horizons, cheap has beaten
  expensive.
- **Leverage amplifies returns.** If value earns a premium, borrowing to hold
  more value should earn more of that premium. That's just multiplication.
- **PSUs are genuinely cheap.** The low multiples are real. The dividends are
  real. You are not imagining the discount.

Each premise is true. The conclusion is still wrong, because the plan stacks
them in a way that quietly assumes three things that are false: that the cheap
stocks are *safe*, that they are *high quality*, and that your borrowing is
*patient*. Strip those assumptions out and the multiplication runs in reverse.

The instinct to "just buy the cheapest thing and lever it" is itself a
*known failure mode* in the literature — it is what happens when you take a
factor premium and apply it naively, without the companion factors that make it
survivable. The academics have a name for the companion factors: **quality** and
**safety**. Leave them out and you are not running the value strategy that won;
you are running the caricature of it that loses.

## The Buffett's Alpha inversion: the one case that proves the point

In 2013, Andrea Frazzini, David Kabiller, and Lasse Pedersen of AQR published
*Buffett's Alpha* (SSRN 2312432; later in the *Financial Analysts Journal*,
2018). They asked a precise question: Buffett's returns are extraordinary — is it
skill, luck, or a replicable formula? Their answer is the most useful thing ever
written for anyone thinking about leveraging value.

They found Buffett's edge decomposes into a handful of factors, and it worked
because of **four legs working together**:

1. **Cheap.** He bought at attractive valuations — the value leg.
2. **Safe.** He bought *low-beta*, low-volatility stocks — companies that don't
   swing violently with the market.
3. **High quality.** He bought profitable, stable, growing, well-run businesses —
   the QMJ ("Quality Minus Junk") leg.
4. **Patient, cheap, non-callable funding.** He leveraged roughly **1.6-to-1** —
   modest — and, critically, financed it with **insurance float**: money that
   cost him *below the T-bill rate* (around 1.7%) and, decisively, **could not be
   called away**. No margin clerk could ever force him to sell at the bottom.

Now line up the cheap-PSU-on-MTF bet against those four legs. It keeps leg 1 and
violates the other three — one by one, in the worst possible direction.

| Leg of the formula | What Buffett actually did (the alpha) | What "cheap PSUs on MTF" does |
|---|---|---|
| **Cheap** | Bought cheap valuations | Buys cheap valuations — the *only* leg it keeps |
| **Safe (low beta)** | Bought low-beta, low-volatility stocks | Buys **high-beta cyclicals** (OMCs, PSU banks, metals, power) — the opposite of safe |
| **High quality** | Bought profitable, growing, well-governed firms | Buys **PSU value-traps** — political interference, capital misallocation, forced dividends/OFS — the opposite of quality |
| **Patient, cheap, non-callable funding** | ~1.6x leverage on ~1.7%, non-callable insurance float | **Callable ~12-15% margin debt** — expensive, and a margin call can force-sell you at the bottom |

Read that table twice. The cheap-PSU-on-MTF plan is not a scaled-down Buffett.
It is **close to the mathematical inverse of Buffett** on three of the four legs
that generated the alpha. It keeps the one leg (cheap) that is necessary but
nowhere near sufficient, and inverts safety, quality, and funding — the three
legs that actually did the work.

And here is the number that should end the fantasy of "extraordinary
leverage-worthy returns": even with all four legs firing, over decades, Buffett's
lifetime **Sharpe ratio was about 0.76.** That is excellent — the best long-run
track record on record — but it is not magic. If the greatest value-plus-leverage
investor in history, doing everything right, earned a Sharpe near 0.76, then the
idea that a *naive cheapest-basket* on *callable* leverage will produce
extraordinary risk-adjusted returns has no support in the evidence. You would be
attempting something harder than what Buffett did, with worse tools, and
expecting a better result.

## Cheapness alone is incomplete: what the factor literature actually says

The deeper problem is that "buy the cheapest stocks" is a misreading of the value
research. The literature is emphatic that cheapness is a *component*, not the
whole engine, and that leveraging it naked is exactly the mistake.

**Betting Against Beta (Frazzini & Pedersen, 2014).** This is the paper that
matters most for the *leverage* part of the plan. Its central finding:
high-beta stocks deliver **poor risk-adjusted returns**, and low-beta stocks
deliver better ones. The profitable strategy is to *lever up low-beta* assets
and short high-beta ones. The cheap-PSU-on-MTF bet does the reverse — it
**levers up high-beta cyclicals.** That is not a neutral choice; it is
documented, by name, as the opposite of what works. You are buying the leg of
the market that pays you *least* per unit of risk, and then adding risk to it.

**Quality Minus Junk (Asness, Frazzini, Pedersen).** The QMJ factor shows that,
holding price constant, high-quality firms — profitable, growing, safe, well-run
— systematically outperform junky ones. Investors *underpay* for quality. The
implication for a value screener is direct: among cheap stocks, the quality ones
are the mispricings and the junky ones are the traps. A raw PE < 6 / PB < 1
screen makes no distinction. It buys the quality and the junk indiscriminately —
and PSU cyclicals are disproportionately on the junk side of that line.

**The Devil in HML's Details (Asness & Frazzini, JPM 2013).** This one is subtle
but load-bearing. It shows the value factor itself is measured and used far
better when combined with other signals — that value works best *jointly with
momentum* and with fresh price information, not as a static "cheapest wins"
sort. The takeaway: even the value premium you are trying to harvest is not
captured by a naive low-multiple screen. The premium lives in a *disciplined,
multi-factor* construction, not in "buy everything under PE 6."

Put together, the message from the people who literally built the value and
leverage factors is: **the load-bearing factors are quality and safety, not
cheapness alone — and value is best used jointly with other signals.** A naive
low-PE/PB PSU basket omits every filter the research says is essential and keeps
only the one that isn't sufficient.

## The Piotroski quality guard — built for exactly this problem

If you want proof that quality-guarding a cheap screen is not my opinion but the
established fix, look at Joseph Piotroski's 2000 paper in the *Journal of
Accounting Research*. Piotroski studied a universe of cheap (high book-to-market)
stocks — the exact universe the PSU plan targets — and asked: can simple
accounting signals separate the winners from the value-traps *within* the cheap
group? His answer was the **9-point F-score**, and it works. Applying it to a
cheap universe meaningfully raised returns and shifted the whole distribution up,
precisely by weeding out the deteriorating names.

What makes the F-score damning for the raw PSU plan is *which* nine things it
checks. Its criteria target, almost line for line, the specific ways PSUs
disappoint shareholders:

- **Decreasing long-term leverage (debt).** Rewards firms cutting debt. Many
  PSUs — power, infrastructure, some banks — carry heavy or rising leverage.
- **No new share issuance.** Penalises dilution. PSUs are serially diluted
  through **Offer-for-Sale (OFS)** as the government sells down its stake to
  raise money — the F-score flags exactly this.
- **Improving return on assets (ROA).** Rewards rising profitability on the asset
  base. PSU capital misallocation — projects chosen for political rather than
  return reasons — shows up as *falling* or stagnant ROA.
- **Improving gross margin and asset turnover.** Rewards operating efficiency
  gains. Overstaffed, mandate-driven PSUs frequently show the opposite.

That is not a coincidence. The F-score's failure modes *are* the PSU failure
modes. This is the mechanistic reason a quality-guarded cheap screen works and a
raw cheapest-first screen doesn't: the guard is specifically constructed to
detect the deterioration that makes a cheap stock a trap, and PSUs are unusually
prone to that deterioration. Screen PE < 6 / PB < 1 with **no F-score filter**
and you are buying the exact stocks Piotroski's method exists to help you avoid.

## The structural PSU discount: why cheap ≠ mispriced

Here is the argument that most retail buyers never confront. The plan assumes
the low multiples are a *mispricing* — that the market is wrong and will
eventually re-rate the stocks up. But there is a strong, reasoned case that the
PSU discount is *structural* — that the market is pricing a set of permanent
disadvantages **correctly**, and the discount will not close because the reasons
for it do not go away.

Consider what a PSU actually is, from a minority shareholder's seat:

- **Government majority ownership and political interference.** The controlling
  shareholder is the state, whose objectives — employment, inflation control,
  strategic priorities, votes — are not "maximise the minority shareholder's
  return." Decisions can and do run against your interest.
- **Capital misallocation.** Investment is often directed by policy rather than
  by return on capital. Loss-making mandates, non-commercial projects, and
  cross-subsidies are features, not bugs.
- **Forced dividends and OFS to plug the fiscal deficit.** When the government
  needs money, PSUs are a source — via special dividends that drain cash the
  business might have reinvested, and via OFS that dilutes or overhangs the
  stock. The controlling shareholder can extract value from the company to fund
  the exchequer.
- **Low free float and disinvestment overhang.** A small tradable float and the
  perpetual possibility of the government selling more shares caps the upside —
  every rally invites another OFS.
- **Energy-transition and ESG risk.** The oil, coal, and thermal-power PSUs that
  dominate the cheap screen sit directly in the path of the energy transition.
  Some of their "cheapness" is the market discounting assets that may be
  structurally challenged over the coming decades.

None of these are temporary. A rational market that knows PSUs will be
periodically milked for dividends, diluted through OFS, run partly for
non-shareholder objectives, and exposed to transition risk **should** assign
them a lower multiple than an otherwise-identical private firm. In that world, a
low PE/PB is not an opportunity — it is the fair price of a permanently
disadvantaged claim on earnings. "Cheap" and "mispriced" are different words,
and the whole leveraged bet rests on assuming they are the same.

That does not mean *every* PSU is uninvestable — some are well-run and genuinely
cheap. It means you cannot infer mispricing from the multiple alone, which is
exactly what the buy-everything-under-PE-6 plan does.

## Concentration and correlation: the basket is one bet, not many

Buyers imagine a PE < 6 / PB < 1 PSU basket as *diversification* — twenty
stocks, surely that's spread out. It is not. Run that screen and watch what it
returns: it clusters, hard, into a handful of **correlated cyclical sectors**:

- **Oil marketing companies** (the BPCL / IOCL / HPCL cluster)
- **PSU banks**
- **Metals and mining**
- **Power and coal**

These are not independent bets. They are macro-linked and they move together. A
spike in crude hammers the OMCs' marketing margins at the same time. A rate
shock or a credit-cycle turn hits the PSU banks together. A global growth
scare knocks metals, mining, and coal in unison. The screen *feels* like twenty
positions; economically it is closer to **two or three correlated macro bets**
wearing twenty tickers.

That is fatal for the diversification story. Diversification only reduces risk
when holdings are imperfectly correlated. A basket that all rises and falls on
the same two or three macro variables gives you the *illusion* of spread with the
*reality* of concentration. And concentration is precisely the thing you cannot
afford when you have added leverage on top — because when the correlated basket
falls, it *all* falls at once, and it takes your margin with it.

## Leverage amplifies the tail: the margin-call math

This is where the three inversions compound into a single catastrophic
mechanism. High-beta + low-quality + callable-funding + concentration don't just
add up — they multiply, and the product is **forced liquidation at the bottom.**

Walk the sequence. You hold a correlated cyclical PSU basket on ~2x MTF. A macro
shock hits — crude spikes, or a rate shock, or a global growth scare — and
because the basket is correlated and high-beta, it doesn't drop 10% politely
over a year. It gaps down **30-40% together, fast.**

Now the leverage turns on you:

- Your equity in the position collapses (leverage multiplies the drawdown on your
  own capital).
- You breach the maintenance margin. The broker issues a **margin call.**
- If you can't top up in cash, the broker **squares off** — sells your positions
  at the market's price, at the bottom, at the worst possible moment.
- What was a *paper* loss you might have ridden out becomes a **realised
  wipeout.** The forced sale converts a temporary drawdown into a permanent loss.
- And through the entire drawdown, **~12-15% annual interest** has been accruing
  *daily* on the borrowed half — you paid to hold the thing all the way down.

| Basket move (correlated, fast) | On cash (unlevered) | On ~2x MTF, after carry |
|---|---|---|
| -20% | -20%, you wait it out | -40%+ on your capital; margin under stress |
| -35% | -35%, still your choice to hold | **Margin call → forced square-off at the bottom; loss realised** |
| Then recovery | You're still holding, you recover | **You're out. You ate the drawdown, missed the recovery, and paid interest through both** |

The cruelty is in the last row. In cash, a cyclical crash is survivable — you
hold, the cycle turns, you recover. On leverage in a correlated basket, the
crash **removes you from the position before the recovery**, at the price you
least wanted, while charging you interest for the privilege. Leverage doesn't
just amplify volatility; against a callable lender it amplifies the **tail** —
and the tail is exactly where correlated high-beta cyclicals live. This is,
plainly, the single most dangerous combination available to a retail investor:
**leverage plus high-beta cyclicals funded by callable debt.** It is the precise
inverse of Buffett's non-callable, low-beta, modest-leverage structure — and it
fails in precisely the way his was engineered to never fail.

## What would actually make it defensible

None of this means deep value is worthless or that PSUs are untouchable. It means
the *naive leveraged cheapest-basket* is the reckless version. There is a
disciplined version, and it is defined entirely by putting back the legs the
naive plan strips out. Here is the honest line between the two:

| Dimension | Reckless version (the trap) | Defensible version (disciplined deep value) |
|---|---|---|
| **Leverage** | ~2x callable MTF at 12-15% | Unleveraged, or low leverage you can service and survive |
| **Selection** | Every stock under PE 6 / PB 1 | **Quality-guarded**: ROE, low promoter pledge, low debt, **Piotroski ≥ 6** |
| **Diversification** | All PSU cyclicals (OMCs, banks, metals, power) | Spread **across uncorrelated sectors**, not one macro bet |
| **Position sizing** | Sized to maximise exposure | Sized to **survive a 40% drawdown with no margin call** |
| **Holding** | "Hold" — but forced out on a margin call | Genuinely held **through the cycle**, on your terms |
| **Funding character** | Callable, expensive | As close to patient and non-callable as you can get (ideally cash) |

The disciplined version is recognisably the *same* factor bet the research
supports: cheap **and** quality **and** safe, diversified, held patiently, with
leverage that can't force-sell you. That's Buffett's four legs, restored. The
reckless version keeps one leg and inverts three. The gap between them is the
entire difference between harvesting the value premium and being destroyed by
the tail.

If you want a single filter that captures the spirit of the fix: **the quality
guard.** A cheap screen without ROE, debt, pledge, and Piotroski checks is not a
value strategy — it's a trap-collection machine.

## The verdict

So, why shouldn't you just buy every PE < 6 / PB < 1 PSU on MTF and hold?

Because the one documented case of value-plus-leverage that worked — Buffett — won
on four legs, and your plan inverts three of them. Because the factor literature
says the load-bearing factors are **quality and safety, not cheapness alone**,
and your screen omits both. Because Piotroski's F-score exists specifically to
separate value from value-traps within the cheap universe, and your screen
skips it — while PSUs are unusually prone to the exact deteriorations it detects.
Because the PSU discount is plausibly **structural, not a mispricing**, so "cheap"
may be the correct price of a permanently disadvantaged claim. Because the basket
is **not diversified** — it's two or three correlated macro bets in disguise.
And because leverage against a **callable** lender turns the correlated tail into
a **forced square-off at the bottom**, converting a survivable drawdown into a
permanent wipeout while charging you 12-15% the whole way down.

The plan keeps the seductive, easy leg — *cheap* — and discards the three legs
that require discipline and cost you upside. That is not a scaled-down version of
what works. It is close to its inverse.

The defensible move is the boring one: quality-guard the screen, diversify across
uncorrelated sectors, size to survive, and either drop the leverage or keep it
low and non-forced. Do that, and you are running deep value. Skip it and lever
the cheapest PSUs, and you are running the caricature the research was written to
warn you about.

## Honest limitations

Two caveats, stated plainly, because the whole point of this post is to be
honest rather than to sell you a story.

**First, the evidence here is factor *theory* and *mechanism*, not an India-PSU
backtest.** Everything cited above — Buffett's Alpha, Betting Against Beta,
Quality Minus Junk, The Devil in HML's Details, Piotroski — is well-documented,
peer-reviewed factor-investing research, and the leverage/quality/safety
mechanism it describes is general and transferable. But this analysis did **not**
independently verify India-specific PSU index return series. If you have heard
about a PSU "lost decade" through roughly 2010-2020, or the sharp PSU rally of
2023-24 and its subsequent correction, treat those as **widely-reported market
context to verify yourself**, not as cited datapoints from this post. I am
deliberately **not** quoting specific Nifty PSE, Nifty CPSE, or BSE PSU index
return figures, because I have not verified them here. Pull the actual index
series before you rely on any historical PSU return number — including any you
may remember.

**Second, this is general information, not a recommendation about any stock or
strategy.** The factor evidence is robust; its application to your situation,
your risk tolerance, and today's specific PSU valuations is not something a blog
post can decide for you.

*General education, not investment advice. Leverage can cause losses exceeding
your margin; MTF interest accrues daily and a margin call can force a sale at the
worst possible time. Factor premia are long-horizon and can underperform for
years. The India-specific PSU return history referenced as context is not
independently verified here — confirm it yourself. Consult a SEBI-registered
investment adviser and a tax professional before acting.*
